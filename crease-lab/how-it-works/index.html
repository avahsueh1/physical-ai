How it works

Crease Lab

An origami robot is a folded sheet plus something that pulls on it. Crease Lab models the sheet as a truss of bars with springy hinges, lets you fold it by hand or let an optimizer pick its shape, teaches a folded tube to crawl with no wheels, and turns the result into a template and servo angles. First time here? The tutorial walks through every bench. After it come the nine ideas it rests on, each with the lines of JavaScript that carry it.

Tutorial

Twenty-five minutes through Fold, Move, Transform and Build, one thing to try at a time, three stages to build it for real, then one electric fold and what copies of it can do

Open Crease Lab in a second tab and keep this page beside it. The five buttons at the top, Fold, Move, Transform, Build and Modules, are five workbenches. Settings are on the left, the 3D view is in the middle, and results are on the right. In the 3D view, drag empty space to turn the camera and scroll to zoom. Reset view puts the camera back.

Words you'll see

WordMeaning
Crease patternThe flat drawing of where the paper folds. It's shown on the green cutting mat.
Mountain / valleyThe two ways a crease can fold. A mountain makes a ridge pointing at you (red, dash-dot). A valley makes a trough (blue, dashed).
StrainHow far the paper has to stretch to take a shape. Real paper barely stretches, so a design with high strain will tear or refuse to fold.
KreslingA tube of triangles that twists as you squash it. The crawling robot is a row of them on its side.
GaitA repeating sequence of moves (squeeze, let go, grip, bend) that makes the body travel.

Fold: shapes from flat paper

  1. Collapse a Miura-ori

    In Fold, pick Miura-ori under Pattern. In Actuation, drag Fold amount from 0% up toward 90%, or tick Cycle fold open and closed and let it run.

    You should see the flat sheet buckle into a zigzag and pack down into a narrow stack. The whole sheet moves together: pull any part and the rest follows. That's why this pattern is used to fold solar panels for launch.

  2. Fold it with your hands

    Untick the cycle box. In the 3D view, double-click one corner of the paper to pin it, then drag the opposite corner. To make the paper keep the shape you give it, tick Creases keep the shape you fold by hand first.

    You should see the creases bend as you pull and the panels stay flat. Without the pin, the whole sheet just slides along with your mouse.

  3. Change a crease

    On the Crease pattern mat under the 3D view, click any red or blue line. In the panel that opens, switch it between Mountain, Valley and Flat (no fold), or drag Fold just this crease. Reset folds undoes every change.

    You should see the 3D sheet fold differently straight away. Flip one Miura crease the wrong way and the sheet fights itself: it can't collapse cleanly any more. The next step measures how badly.

  4. See where the paper stretches

    Pick Miura-ori, press Reset folds, then press Strain map under the 3D view. Set Fold amount to about 60% and watch the Max strain number at the top of the 3D view. The colour bar runs from white (no stretch) to red (3% stretch).

    Now click any one red or blue crease on the mat and switch it to the opposite type (Mountain to Valley, or the other way round).

    You should see the sheet stay mostly white or pale yellow at first, with Max strain around 1%. After the flip, patches turn orange and red and Max strain jumps to 3–5%. That one crease now disagrees with its neighbours, so the panels around it can't all stay flat and have to stretch. Real paper won't stretch that much, so it would crumple or refuse to fold. Reset folds puts it back.

  5. Let the computer pick the shape

    Back on Miura-ori, go to Optimize the design. Set Goal to Fold most compactly and press Run search. Each dot is one design it tried and simulated, and the line is the best so far. When it stops, press Use best design.

    You should see the Geometry sliders jump to new values and a sheet that packs smaller than the default. The search is CMA-ES, explained in section 04.

  6. Make a tower that snaps

    Pick Kresling tower. Now the Actuation control is a cable: drag Tendon pull, or press Pulse tendon for a quick tug. Then press Run sweep in Characterize.

    You should see the tower twist as it shortens. The sweep plots how hard it pushes back as it's squashed. If the curve dips below zero, the tower snaps shut and stays shut on its own (it's bistable). Try a different Twist per cell and run the sweep again.

Bring your own design

Press Open design… (top right of the bench), or drop a file anywhere on the bench. It opens three kinds of crease pattern: a .fold file (Origami Simulator, Rabbit Ear, ORIPA's FOLD export), an ORIPA .cp file, or an .svg drawing with red lines for mountains, blue for valleys and black for the outer edge, the same colours Origami Simulator uses. Lines are split where they cross and the panels are found automatically, so a drawing made in Inkscape or Illustrator works as long as the lines meet. A pattern drawn at a large size (in pixels or millimetres) is scaled down to fit the bench. It appears as a new button under Pattern; raise Fold to fold it.

Save design downloads one .creaselab.json file with everything on every tab: the pattern and any creases you changed, the crawler and its gait, the transformer, and the Build choices. Open it again later with Open design… to carry on where you left off.

Move: a robot with no wheels

  1. Watch it crawl

    Click Move. A Kresling tube lies on its side with four cables running through it. In Gait, leave Start from on Inchworm.

    You should see the body squeeze and relax over and over while it inches forward. The feet are directional scales: they slide forward easily but grip backward, like the hairs on a ski skin. So each squeeze pulls the tail up and each relax pushes the head on.

  2. Drive it yourself

    In Motion primitives, drag Contract to squeeze, then drag Bend and pick a Bend toward direction. The gait pauses while you do this. Press Back to the gait when you're done.

    You should see that Contract shortens the whole body, while Bend shortens one side so the body curls. Bending is how the robot steers.

  3. Take away the grip

    In Feet, choose Plain paper, then press Score this gait.

    You should see the body stretch and squeeze but go nowhere. The path chart is nearly a dot. With the same friction in every direction, the front and back slip equally and cancel out. Crawling needs something lopsided: scales, or anchors that grip on command (Switchable anchors, used by the Two-anchor crawl gait).

  4. Teach it a better gait

    Put Feet back on Directional scales. In Learn a gait, set Goal to Crawl fastest and press Run search. It tries many timings and squeeze amounts, crawling each one in the simulator. Then press Use learned gait and Score this gait.

    You should see the phases in the Gait card change and a longer line on the path chart. You can also edit a phase by hand, or press Add phase, and score it again.

Transform: one robot, four shapes

  1. Switch body shapes

    Click Transform. This is the Move crawler with a hinged neck and a head cell, standing on a long battery sled under the shoulder. Under Body shape, press Tower, then Compact, then Crawl.

    You should see the tail and sled pads turn blue (gripping) and the head swing up until it points at the sky. The Camera number climbs from about 1.1 cm to nearly 3 cm. Compact squeezes the body to about half its length with the neck locked straight, and Crawl inches it along like the Move bench.

  2. Aim the head

    Press Reach and drag Neck angle.

    You should see the head follow the slider and hold where you leave it, because a servo is steering the neck tendons. The two short lines across the hinge are those tendons: the top one glows when it lifts, the bottom one when it lowers. Coming down from upright is slow (about 6 s), because near vertical the head's weight doesn't help.

  3. Run a mission and judge it

    Press Run mission. The robot starts folded, crawls, raises its camera, lowers its head to press a target, and folds up again. The table fills in step by step. Then read Is transforming worth it?

    You should see the transformer win on camera height (about 2.4×) and on holding a tip off the floor, which a crawl-only robot can't do. It loses on crawl speed (about 84% as fast) and on how short it packs. Try a lighter battery or fewer body cells and run it again to see which numbers move.

Build: the real thing, in three stages

  1. Stage 1: the crawler, tethered

    Click Build and keep Robot to build on Crawler and Power on Tethered. It builds the robot and gait you set up in Move. Flip between Body strip, Plates, Trays and 3D robot in the middle, and read the Weight check, Servo plan, Wiring, Parts, Controller code and Build steps on the right. Press Download build kit (.zip).

    You should see three A4 pages: the body strip (score, fold and glue it into a tube), the plates, and the trays that hold the servos and double as foot pads. The weight check says the two servos alone weigh about twice the paper body, and the simulator predicts the crawl slows to about two-thirds of the bare body's pace.

  2. Stage 2: let it carry its own battery

    Switch Power to On board. A 1S LiPo, a 5 V boost converter and the board move onto a tray under the head.

    You should see the weight rise to about 95 g and the predicted crawl drop a little more, to just over half of bare. The weight check also shows the same weight all on the tail would leave barely a quarter, which is why the battery rides at the head. Build Stage 1 first: Stage 2 reuses its servo zeros.

  3. Stage 3: the transformer

    Switch Robot to build to Transformer. It upgrades the Stage 2 robot: the battery moves to a sled under the last plate, a third servo becomes the neck hinge, and a card box with an optional camera becomes the head.

    You should see a fourth page (the head box), a servo plan for each shape, and a weight check that runs Tower, Reach, Compact and Crawl with every real part's weight in place. The camera rises from 33 mm to about 81 mm. The code takes c, w, t and r over the serial monitor, and a button on the sled runs the whole mission.

Modules: one electric fold, then many

  1. Fold one module

    Click Modules. The middle shows one module from the side: two card panels and a crease, the left panel clamped to a table. SMA wire is selected. Press Fold, wait, then Release.

    You should see the wire turn red as it heats to about 100 °C and the panel swing up to about 80° in just over half a second. After you release it, nothing happens for a couple of seconds while the wire cools, then the band on the back pulls the fold open. The Fold test card plots exactly that: fold angle and wire temperature over time.

  2. Try the other two pullers

    Pick Servo, then SMA spring, and fold each. Then read Servo or shape-memory? on the right, which runs all three on the same hinge.

    You should see the servo close and open in about a second, but weigh 10 g a module; the wire and the spring weigh 0.4 g but take 4 to 6 s a cycle, most of it cooling. Now pick SMA wire again and drag Return band preload down to 0.5 N: the warning says it no longer opens, because a cold wire has to be stretched back out by something.

  3. Chain copies into a crawler

    Switch the view to Crawler and press 4×. In the Crawler card, try Modules 1 to 6, the Wave and Together gaits, and Plain feet. Then Build it: pick One module or The crawler and press Download kit (.zip).

    You should see three wire modules crawl about 50 mm a cycle (7.4 mm/s); the servo version goes three and a half times faster but weighs 14 times more. With plain feet it goes nowhere. The kit has the template, the parts, the wiring with one MOSFET and one current-limiting resistor per module, and code that never heats a wire for more than 10 s or before it has cooled.

If something looks wrong

The sheet flew off or crumpled. Press Defaults on Geometry, or pick the pattern again to rebuild it. Very low damping or stretch stiffness makes it wobbly.

Panels pass through each other. The simulator has no collision between panels, so a sheet folded far enough, or pushed by hand, can fold straight through itself. Yoshimura does this early, because as a flat sheet it collapses fast. Real paper would stop where layers touch. Keep the fold amount lower if it bothers you.

I can't click the Kresling creases. That's on purpose. The tower's folds come from its geometry, so the mat only shows the strip to cut out.

Nothing moves in Move. Check that Pause isn't pressed, and press Back to the gait if you were driving it by hand.

The numbered sections below explain what the simulator is doing behind each of those buttons.

01

A sheet is a truss with springy hinges

The bar-and-hinge model

Simulating paper as a continuous shell is slow and fussy. The bar-and-hinge model, used by MERLIN (Liu and Paulino) and Origami Simulator (Ghassaei, Demaine and Gershenfeld), keeps only what matters for folding. Every crease pattern is cut into triangles. Each triangle edge becomes a bar that resists stretching, and each edge shared by two triangles becomes a hinge with a rotational spring. Mountain and valley creases get soft hinges; the diagonals that split a flat panel get stiff ones, so the panel stays nearly flat.

Bars are linear springs with a damper along their own direction. The stiffness is axial / L0, so a short bar and a long bar of the same material stretch by the same fraction under the same load.

  1. The bar's unit direction and how fast its two ends move apart along it.
  2. Spring plus damper: k(L − L0) pulls the bar back to its rest length and c·v bleeds off oscillation. The damping ratio is 0.45 of critical.
  3. Equal and opposite forces on the two ends, so bars never push the sheet as a whole.

The hinge needs the fold angle between its two triangles and how that angle changes when any of the four corners moves. The angle comes from the two face normals; the gradient is the closed form from Liu and Paulino (2017). Valley folds are positive, as in the FOLD file format. A test in development compared this gradient with finite differences and they agreed to 10−10.

  1. Signed angle from the two face normals m and n, measured about the hinge axis: 0 when flat, +180° folded fully as a valley.
  2. Unwrap against the previous angle, so a crease folded past 180° does not suddenly jump to −180°.
  3. Moving a wing corner straight off the hinge changes the angle at a rate of 1 / (its distance from the hinge), along the face normal.
  4. The two hinge corners take the rest, weighted by where the wings sit along the hinge, so the four gradients sum to zero and the hinge never pushes the sheet as a whole.
02

Folding is a torque on every crease

Targets, not positions

Nothing in the model says where the paper should end up. Each hinge only has a target angle, and its spring pulls toward that target. The fold slider sets every valley's target to +180° × the fold amount and every mountain's to −180° × the fold amount, the same trick Origami Simulator uses. The bars then decide what shape is possible.

  1. A valley heads for +π·p, a mountain for −π·p. rest is 0 for a flat sheet; for the Kresling tower it is the angle of the built tower, so the tower is stress-free as built.
  2. Flat panel lines aim to stay flat.

The hinge force is the torque −k(θ − θtarget) times the gradient from 01, applied to all four corners. The model steps forward in time with semi-implicit Euler, using a time step set from the stiffest node so it stays stable.

  1. Torque from the rotational spring. h.k is the crease stiffness times the crease length, so long creases are proportionally stiffer.
  2. The same torque times each corner's gradient. This is exactly the force that lowers the spring's energy fastest.
A side effect worth knowing

Asking every crease for the same angle is not quite what a Miura-ori wants: its zigzag creases and straight creases fold at different rates. The creases fight a little, and panels stretch by about 1.3% midway through the fold, then relax as it closes. A real Miura folds with no stretch at all, so read the strain map as "how hard the creases disagree", not as tearing.

03

A hand, and paper that remembers

Direct manipulation in 3D

Dragging the paper casts a ray from the mouse into the scene, finds the triangle it hits and the exact point on it, and attaches a spring from that point to the cursor. The point is a weighted mix of the triangle's three corners, so you can grab the middle of a panel, not just a corner. Double-clicking pins a corner in place, which is what you need to fold one half of a sheet over the other.

  1. A spring and damper between the grabbed point and the cursor. Velocities are mixed with the same barycentric weights as positions.
  2. A hand, not a vice: the pull is capped. Without the cap, dragging fast stretches the bars by 10% or more and the sheet looks torn.
  3. Letting go copies each crease's current angle into its target, so the fold stays where you left it, like creased paper. You can switch this off on the page.

Clicking a crease on the cutting mat selects it. You can flip it between mountain, valley and flat, or hold just that crease at an angle while everything else follows the fold slider.

04

Let the optimizer choose the shape

CMA-ES on simulated designs

Each pattern has a few continuous dimensions (panel width, zigzag length and sector angle for the Miura; cell height and twist for the Kresling). The optimizer works in a unit box over those, decodes a point into a design, builds it, folds or squeezes it to equilibrium, and scores it. There is no gradient through the simulator, so it uses CMA-ES, which only needs scores.

  1. Every candidate is a fresh simulation, run until the kinetic energy has settled. That is about 11 to 13 ms each on a laptop, so 12 generations take about a second.
  2. CMA-ES minimises, so fitness is minus the score, plus a penalty for stepping outside the box (the design itself is clamped back inside).

The goals are deliberately physical. For sheets: fold to the smallest footprint with a penalty for stretched panels, or fold with the least stretch. For the Kresling tower: the longest stroke for a given pull, or the strongest second stable state, which the page measures by pushing the lid down step by step and recording the force (a dip below zero means the tower snaps shut and stays there).

  1. The new mean is a weighted average of the best half of the candidates.
  2. The evolution path for the step size: how consistently the mean has been moving.
  3. The covariance learns the shape of good directions, from the long-run path (rank one) and this generation's winners (rank μ).
  4. Steps grow when progress is consistent and shrink when it is random. This is Hansen's standard update, written out for small dimensions with a Jacobi eigensolver.
05

Crawling needs an asymmetry

Gravity, a floor, friction and cables

The robot is a Kresling tube lying on its side: rigid plates between the cells, a foot pad under each end plate, and four cables running the whole length through an eyelet in every plate. Cables are internal forces, so pulling them can never move the robot's centre of mass. Only the ground can. That is the whole problem of locomotion without wheels: a body that stretches and shrinks symmetrically on uniform friction goes nowhere, and the page's "plain paper" gait shows exactly 0% progress per cycle.

Something has to be asymmetric. With directional feet (like the hairs on a climbing skin), the contacts slide forward easily and grip backwards. With switchable anchors, the robot chooses which end grips in each phase.

  1. Each contact's velocity is split into a part along the body and a part across it. Directional feet use a low coefficient forwards (0.12) and a high one backwards (1.2).
  2. Coulomb friction as an impulse: friction removes at most μ·N·dt of sliding speed per step and never reverses it. So a foot either sticks completely or slides, and the step stays stable even with an anchor's μ of 40.

A cable through eyelets pulls each pair of neighbouring eyelets together with its tension. The eyelets sit on the plates, between the corners and the centre, so the pull goes into the plates rather than straight along a crease, where a stiff bar would simply absorb it.

  1. Tension along the straight run between two eyelets.
  2. Each eyelet is a fixed weighted mix of its plate's corners, so the force is shared among them with the same weights.
What it took to get this right

The first version routed cables along the tube's own creases, and pulling them did almost nothing. The next let the end plates rest on their corners, and a one-sided pull just tipped them over. The model only behaved once each plate was made a thick rigid disc (two hub points just off its plane), each end got a foot pad with four contact points, and gravity was heavy enough to keep the pads down.

06

Gaits are timed primitives

Contract, bend, twist, anchor, release

A gait is a short list of phases. Each phase sets a cable pattern and which feet grip. Contract pulls all four cables; bend adds extra pull on one side; twist comes for free when every cell has the same handedness; release just lets the creases spring the body back. Between phases the tensions ease over a third of a second, like a servo moving.

  1. Cable tension is a contraction everyone shares plus a cosine-shaped extra pull toward the bend direction.
  2. A smoothstep blend from the previous phase's tensions to this phase's.
  3. Anchors switch at the start of a phase, all at once.

The same CMA-ES from 04 can tune a gait: it keeps the order of phases and their anchors, and searches each phase's duration, contraction and bend. Every candidate is crawled for three cycles (one warm-up, two scored), about 20 ms each.

07

From cable stroke to servo angle

What a real robot needs from the simulator

Hobby servos hold an angle, not a force. So the one number the build needs from the simulator is how far each cable pair has to shorten in each phase. The Build tab pulls each phase's cable pattern until the body settles, measures the cable paths, scales them to the chosen size and divides by the spool radius.

  1. Shortening of each cable, averaged into a left pair and a right pair (one servo each), in millimetres at the chosen plate size.
  2. Arc length to angle: θ = stroke / spool radius.
  3. The spool that keeps the largest stroke inside 85% of a 180° servo. For the default robot at 30 mm that is a 22 mm radius: the 59 mm stroke becomes 150°.

The same tab draws the fold template at true scale (the flat strip comes from unrolling the tower's triangles, whose three side lengths fix the strip's slant), the plates with their eyelet holes, a wiring diagram and parts list, and an Arduino sketch whose phase table holds these angles and timings. Everything downloads as one zip.

08

One body, four shapes

The Transform bench

The idea behind the bench: the selling point of an origami robot may not be that it beats a specialised robot at one job, but that one compact body can change between a few useful shapes. The target here is the four-mode inspection robot from a concept brief on reconfigurable origami robots. Compact stows it, Crawl moves it, Tower lifts a camera, and Reach holds a tip on a target. The brief's test is that every change of shape should unlock something a fixed-form robot can't do nearly as well, so the bench measures that too.

A Kresling tube won't bend

The obvious first try was to make the Move crawler curl. Pull only the top cables to lift its head, or only one side's to swing it. It doesn't work, and the reason is geometric. Every plate is a rigid ring, and every panel between two rings is a triangle of fixed-length bars. Tilting one ring against the next would stretch some of those bars, but twisting and shortening stretches none. So a one-sided pull just shortens the whole body. With twice the usual pull it shrank to 34% of its length while the head moved 0.1 cm sideways. Making only the end plates rigid, or softening the panels until they strained 10%, changed nothing. The tube is a good contractor and a poor bender.

Real cable-driven origami arms bend because their panels give a little, or because the robot is built from modules joined by real hinges. The bench takes the second route. It keeps Kresling cells for the body, which only has to shorten, and adds one hinged neck module between the body and a single Kresling head cell.

  1. Each pin is braced to every corner and hub of both plates, so each plate is rigidly fixed to both pins. Two shared points leave exactly one free motion: rotation about the line through them. The frame bars are eight times stiffer than the paper's, because a hinge is plastic. At paper stiffness the pins spread from 1.9 to 2.55 apart under load and the hinge went floppy.

The hinge needed three more fixes before it lifted anything, and each one is a real design rule. First, the body cables run the full length, and pulling them to work the hinge mostly crushed the Kresling cells on either side. So the neck has its own pair of short tendons, the way a servo would sit at an elbow. Second, the hinge had nothing under it. Closing its top made a V, and the joint sank to the floor while both halves tilted 40°. A long sled under the shoulder plate fixed that. Third, the reaction to lifting the head tipped the shoulder back 61° over a short pad, even with weight added. The answer was the same sled made long (±1.5 cell lengths) with the battery on it, and no weight on the invisible construction points (hubs and pins), so the head weighs what a camera module would.

The neck angle is held by a PID servo that pulls the top tendon to raise the head and the bottom one to lower it. In Compact and Crawl, both tendons stay snug so the hinge is locked straight.

  1. Proportional, derivative and integral terms on the hinge angle, plus a feed-forward term that holds up the head's weight. The weight's pull is largest when the head lies flat and vanishes when it stands upright, hence the cosine.
  2. One signed command becomes two tendon tensions, because a tendon can only pull. In Compact and Crawl the target is 0°, so both tendons are held at 3, which locks the hinge.

Is transforming worth it?

The mission runs the brief's five steps in order: Deploy (from Compact), Move (four inchworm cycles), Inspect (Tower), Intervene (Reach at 45°) and Recover (Compact). The baseline is the plain Move crawler with the same five Kresling cells and no neck, given the same pull.

Verdict

For this mission, yes. The neck lifts the camera 2.4× higher and holds a tip off the floor, which the fixed-form crawler can't do at all. The cost is 16% slower crawling (the head and sled are dead weight while it moves) and a much longer stowed package, because the hinge and head don't fold. For a job that only needs crawling or tight storage, the plain crawler is the better robot. That's the brief's decision rule, measured.

09

Three stages to a real robot

Weight, power and the transformer kit

A paper robot is light, and everything that makes it move is not. At the default size (plate radius 30 mm), the body strip and plates weigh about 19 g. Two MG90S servos with their spools, risers and tray weigh 37 g, and they sit on the tail. The simulator's crawler had been carrying none of that. So the Build tab now estimates every part's weight and asks the simulator what the load does before you buy anything.

The paper weight comes from the template itself: triangle areas times the cardstock's grams per square metre, and plate areas times the card's weight. Parts use typical catalogue weights. Each tray's load then goes onto the plate it hangs from, as a fraction of the simulated body's own weight, and the gait runs again.

  1. The tray loads become extra downward force on the tail and head plate nodes, scaled by how heavy they are compared with the paper body. Everything else (cables, friction, gait) is the Move bench unchanged.

What the weight check found

Where the weight goes matters as much as how much there is. With the inchworm gait the tail has to slide forward on every contraction, so a heavy tail drags. That's why Stage 2 puts the battery and board under the head and leaves the servos at the tail. The two-anchor gait behaves the other way: extra weight makes its gripping feet hold better, and its speed goes up under load (9.7% bare, 14.6% with the Stage 2 load). A cheaper SG90 saves 9 g and wins back a few points (15.5% tethered).

Two geometry problems showed up only when the kit was laid out at real scale. First, the default gait pulls 59 mm of cable, which needs a 22 mm spool to fit in a servo's travel. Second, the transformer's Compact needs 81 mm, which needs a 30 mm spool, and a spool that size would scrape the floor with its shaft at plate-centre height. The kit now raises the servo risers until the spool clears the floor by 8 mm, and prints the riser height.

Stage 3: the transformer, with real weights

The transformer kit starts from the Stage 2 crawler and adds three things. A sled under the last plate carries the battery and board, 25 mm back and 55 mm forward of the plate; it is the simulator's shoulder pad, made asymmetric so it can't hit the tail tray in Compact. A third servo lies on its side 28.5 mm in front of that plate and is itself the neck hinge, so the simulator's neck tendons and their PID controller become one servo told an angle. A card box becomes the head. Putting every part's weight into the transformer simulator (140 g in all, 7.2 times the 19 g paper body) gave these results.

What was checked, and what wasn't

No Arduino toolchain could be downloaded here, so every generated sketch was compiled against stand-in Arduino headers in both styles real cores use (macros on the Nano, C++ templates on the ESP32 and Pico), then run on a simulated clock to check the servo angles over time. That caught two sequencing bugs: the neck had moved while the cables were tight, and Compact had started before the head was back down. An independent review of the generated kits then found more, all now fixed: servos fed from the laptop's USB port during uploads (the kits now have a removable 5V link), no step to set the boost converter to 5 V, a battery and converter too small for two stalling servos, a charger set too fast for the cell, servos that could snap after a pause, a sled drawn the wrong way round, and a camera that looked at the ceiling in Tower (it now looks out of the head's underside, which faces forward when the head is up). It is not the same as flashing a board. The part weights are catalogue values, the card neck brackets are untested, and the speeds above assume the simulator's friction and crease stiffness, which Stage 1's measurements are there to calibrate.

One fold, three motors

The Modules bench

The other benches start from a whole robot. This one starts from the smallest thing that moves on its own: one crease that something electric can close. Two card panels share the crease. A puller runs across the inside of the fold between two anchors, A on the fixed panel and B on the moving one, each on a short post (the standoff). A rubber band on the outside opens it again, and a card tab stops it bending backwards. Copies of that module, joined end to end, make a crawler.

A hinge, not a sheet

One module has one degree of freedom, the fold angle θ, so it gets its own small simulation instead of the bar-and-hinge model. The moving panel's inertia, the paper crease (a torsion spring, stiffer for heavier card), the band, gravity and damping act on θ. The puller's pull becomes a torque through the geometry: it pulls along the straight line between A and B, and the torque is the pull times how fast that line shortens as θ grows. That lever arm starts at the standoff height and grows as the fold closes, which is why a fold is easy to hold shut and hard to start.

Three pullers

The servo winds a thread onto a spool. It has a stall torque, a top speed that falls under load, and a current that rises with load (SG90 and MG90S catalogue figures). The shape-memory wire is Nitinol: cold it is soft (martensite), and heated past about 70 °C it turns into a stiff phase (austenite) that remembers being 4% shorter. The spring is the same metal coiled, so it shortens by far more than 4% but pulls far less, computed with the usual coil-spring formulas in shear.

  1. The wire is thin enough to be one temperature: electrical heating in, still-air cooling out. The cooling coefficient is set so the model's cooling times match Dynalloy's Flexinol table (0.8 s at 0.10 mm, 2.0 s at 0.15 mm, 5.5 s at 0.25 mm), and it scales as one over the diameter.
  2. How much of the wire has turned hot (ξ) follows the temperature, with hysteresis: it changes on the way up between As and Af (68–78 °C) and on the way down between Ms and Mf (52–42 °C). Stress raises all four, about 0.15 °C per MPa, so a heavily loaded wire needs to be hotter.
  3. Changing phase takes heat (16 J/g), which is why the temperature curve flattens near 77 °C while the fold closes.

The last lines of that step are what make the return band necessary. A cold wire doesn't spring back: it stays the length it was until something stretches it, at about 35 MPa. With the fold closed its lever arm is long, so the band, working on a short arm round the outside of the crease, has to be about three times stronger than you'd guess. Below about 1.1 N the default wire module folds once and stays folded, and just above that it takes over 6 s to open. A spring needs only a weak band, since it takes so little to stretch it.

The circuit and controller are the ones in the generated kit. A resistor in series with each wire is sized so that fully switched on, the current is Dynalloy's one-second current for that diameter. The controller heats at full power for as long as the heat balance says it takes to get fully hot, then drops to a holding power that keeps it about 10 °C above its (stress-raised) Af, around 100 °C. It never stays on longer than 10 s. The resistor is there for safety: even if a MOSFET fails stuck on, the wire can only ever carry its rated current. It costs energy, though. In the crawler about two-thirds of the heating power goes into the resistor.

The servo wins on speed, control and energy, and the shape-memory pullers win on weight, by a factor of 14 for the crawler. (The servo times are set by the sketch, which never turns a servo faster than 200° a second.) Tethered to a laptop, the servo is the better choice. For a robot that carries its own battery, every gram of puller is a gram the folds have to lift, and that is where shape-memory pays off. The wire uses about twice the servo's energy per millimetre crawled, mostly in its safety resistor, even though the crawler's arch stop means each fold needs only a short wire (65 mm, cut so its full shortening just reaches the stop). The spring is three times worse than the servo, partly because the stop blocks it before it has finished closing.

Copies in a row

In the crawler every fold bends the same way, so the strip is always an arch standing on its two ends. The model is quasi-static. Each hinge runs its own simulation, and the strip's shape follows from the angles. When the ends move closer together, or further apart, one end has to slide, and it is the one that's easier to slide.

  1. The weight on each end comes from where the centre of mass sits between them. Folding pulls the rear end forward or the front end back, whichever costs less friction. With feet that grip four times harder backwards than forwards, the rear slides forward as it folds and the front slides forward as it opens, so every cycle gains the whole change in length. With the same grip both ways, the ends share the movement and it goes nowhere, which is what the plain feet show.

An earlier version simulated the strip freely, with inertia and contact. Its crawling speed changed several-fold with an arbitrary damping setting, because the light paper hopped every time a fold snapped shut, and it reported more distance per cycle than the arch even gets shorter by. The quasi-static model can't do that: three folds to 40° shorten the strip by 50 mm, and it crawls just under that, 49.8 mm a cycle. It ignores momentum, so a real fast servo crawler may scoot or bounce in ways it misses.

What was checked, and what wasn't

The shape-memory numbers come from Dynalloy's published Flexinol table (resistance, the current for a one-second contraction, recommended pull and cooling times) and textbook values for Nitinol's moduli, heat capacity and latent heat. The paper crease stiffness and the friction of the feet are estimates. Every generated sketch (3 pullers × 3 boards × one module or a crawler) compiles against stand-in Arduino headers and was run on a simulated clock. That run found that the first version refused to heat any wire in the first 3.6 s after power-on. It also checked that a fold held on is cut off at 10 s and never re-heated before it has cooled, and that servos never jump once running (at power-up they move to their zero angle).

An independent review of the first version found more, all now fixed:

  • Short or thick wire could be pulsed at ten times its rated current, with nothing in the hardware to stop a MOSFET stuck on (hence the series resistor).
  • The wire was routed round 1 mm paperclip pins, far below the bend radius Dynalloy asks for. It now runs straight, round one 10 mm post, or off the panel.
  • Plastic straw posts were suggested next to a 100 °C wire.
  • The servo gait timings assumed a faster servo than the sketch allows.
  • The power ratings and fuses were fixed values instead of being sized from the current.
  • The start button had no debounce.
  • An ESP32 pin pulsed at boot.
  • Hot-wire stress had no limit.
  • The end stops were springy enough to overshoot.

Nothing here has been built yet.

Limits

Honest caveats

  • Nothing here has been checked against a real fold or a real robot yet. Crease stiffness in particular is a guess; the page tells you to measure the built body's contraction and tune the simulator to match.
  • The units are made up. Gravity, friction and stiffness were tuned by hand until the crawler behaved plausibly, so absolute speeds and forces mean little.
  • Driving every crease toward the same angle overstates Miura strain (02).
  • Steering is weak: about 2° per cycle, and the two-anchor crawl drifts about 1° right per cycle, apparently from how the alternating cells twist.
  • The Transform bench's hinge frames and battery are modelling choices, not measurements: the frames are 8× stiffer than paper and the battery's weight is a slider. Its comparison is one mission against one baseline, and the fixed-form crawler wasn't tuned for that mission.
  • There is no self-collision. Panels can pass through each other past about 60% contraction, and the hand can push the sheet through itself.
  • The optimizer often ends at the edge of a parameter's range, which means the range, not the physics, is choosing the answer there.
  • The generated Arduino sketches have been compiled against stand-in headers and run on a simulated clock (09), not compiled with a real board toolchain or run on hardware.
  • The weight check uses catalogue part weights and assumes the simulator's friction is right. Weigh your parts, and calibrate the simulator with Stage 1 before trusting its Stage 2 and 3 speeds.
  • The Modules bench simulates one hinge at a time and the crawler quasi-statically: modules don't load each other's hinges, and momentum is ignored. Its shape-memory model is a simplified two-phase model fitted to one maker's data sheet, so expect to tune heating times and band strength on the real wire.
  • It is plain JavaScript on one thread: fine for exploring, too slow for serious reinforcement learning. CreaseLab.CrawlerEnv has the reset/step shape you would port to JAX or PyTorch.

Reproduce

No build step, no install

Open the page. Everything is exposed as window.CreaseLab, so the numbers above can be rerun from the browser console:

const CL = window.CreaseLab;
CL.evaluateGait(CL.defaultRobot(), CL.GAITS.inchworm, { cycles: 3 });   // perCycle 0.198
const s = CL.buildMesh(CL.buildPattern('miura', CL.defaultParams('miura')));
s.damping = 2; CL.foldSweep(s, 19, 0.95);                              // footprint and strain vs fold
const env = new CL.CrawlerEnv({}, { feet: 'anchors' });                // reset() / step(action)
const report = CL.runMission();                                        // Transform: five steps + fixed-form baseline

The same files also load in Node (require('./web/build.js') pulls in the rest), which is how the tables here were made.
